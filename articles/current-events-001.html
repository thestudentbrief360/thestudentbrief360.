Amphibian Extinction Crisis

By The Student Brief 360 • 2026 • Informational Brief

When we think about endangered animals, we don’t usually think about frogs and salamanders. But in reality, amphibians are in crisis. The endangerment and extinction of amphibian species is a permanent loss of a fascinating, unique, ancient legacy of the natural world. “The faster amphibian populations decline, the more we should be worried about our ecosystem as a whole”. (Seale). Amphibians are indicator species, and they rely on the outside environment to regulate their bodies. They are more susceptible to toxins, so this means that they are the “canary in the coalmine” for our ecosystems, warning us of potential issues.

“The faster amphibian populations decline, the more we should be worried about our ecosystem as a whole”.

Based on research and records, Chiriqui harlequin frog, sharp-snouted day frog, wry lip brittle-belly frog, Jalpa false brook salamander are four amphibian species confirmed extinct since 2004. (Seale). As many as 222 amphibian species could have already gone extinct, and 2,873 are in danger of extinction, says the second Global Amphibian Assessment. It has also shown amphibians to be threatened wherever they are found around the world. Most threatened species are found in countries with mountainous tropical forests and on tropical islands Habitat loss affects 93% of threatened amphibian species, specifically, forest loss (84%). The main cause of forest loss is converting them into agricultural land, which threatens over 2,000 species.

But the biggest contender is climate change. “As humans drive changes in climate and habitats, amphibians are becoming climate captives, unable to move very far to escape extreme heat, wildfires, drought, hurricanes, and other effects of climate change” - Jennifer Luedtke Swanby (Re:wild Manager of Species Partnerships). Amphibians are highly vulnerable to climate change. As they have highly permeable skin which relies on moisture for breathing and hydration, they are especially sensitive to shifts in temperature and humidity. (Amphibian Ark). Climate change causes longer dry seasons, less rain, and the resulting changes to habitats. Other impacts include, increased incidents of storms, flooding and temperature extremes. But, unfortunately, the threat list doesn’t stop here for these poor creatures.

Sometimes their own bodies become their enemies. Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, or Bd. chytrid for short. A fungus pandemic that is threatening 600 species, and putting 75 more in danger. And, Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans, or Bsal for short; a disease that specifically threatens salamanders. For more than half of the amphibian species currently classified as Critically Endangered, disease is the primary cause. (Amphibian Ark). The Neotropics suffered great amphibian declines due to chytridimycosis (discovered in 1993). The only cure? Holding the animals captive to avoid spreading the disease.

Even though human intervention does little for these highly threatened creatures, we can do our part by being aware of this crisis. The IUCN SSC Amphibian Specialist Group (ASG), coordinated the efforts of scientists worldwide to evaluate the status of all known amphibian species and to develop Red List extinction risk assessments for as many as possible. “Thanks to the Red List Data, almost 1,200 Key Biodiversity Areas have been identified for amphibians worldwide” (Seale). The State of the World Amphibians report: comprises more than a decade of research on amphibians by over 1,000 amphibian experts. It has assessed over 8,000 species worldwide. Hopefully with the help of scientists, conservationists, and the public, we can prevent the loss of this extraordinary lifeform.
